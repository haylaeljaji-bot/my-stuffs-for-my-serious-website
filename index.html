    #architecture #fictions #computation

    Worked on during my time at CoDEx Lab at McGill University under Theodora Vardouli, our first speculative reenactment is HIDECS 2 (Alexander and Manheim 1962). Written in FORTRAN and ran on the IBM 709 of the MIT Computation Center, the system “decomposed” large lists of design requirements into a hierarchy of smaller requirement groups by examining relationships (“interactions”) between them. It computed for designers a sequence by which to address and “recompose” their design responses to these smaller groupings of requirements. We have designed and built a physical prototype for the hardware, coded the FORTRAN algorithm of the 1962 program in Java, and developed a speculative custom workflow that automates the “recomposition” process through the ChatGPT and DeepAI APIs. This workflow translates the decomposed groups of verbal statements (“design requirements”) into stylized generated images (“diagrams”) and interactively combines these diagrams to produce a final image of a design.

    #architecture #feminism

    The Ottawa Mosque at 251 Northwestern Avenue in Ottawa, Ontario, was completed in 1977 by architect Ghazi Anwar Asad as the city’s first purpose-built mosque. It choreographs gender from the moment of entry; men pass through the prominent, 15-foot front glazed double doors into the main hall, while women enter from a recessed 3-foot steel side single door, remove our shoes in the staircase landing, and walk up to a balcony above. From there, most of us see the imam not directly but through a digital screen, our participation mediated by technology. Muslims often emphasize Islam as the only Abrahamic religion whose text has remained unchanged, ‘uncorrupted’ by revisions. Yet, it can be argued that Islam has been rewritten continuously through interpretation: its meanings reshaped by jurisprudence, cultural practice, and architecture. The Ottawa Mosque, then, is an interpretive object to think about how faith moves from text to space, and how the built environment participates in redefining the limits of inclusion, participation, and visibility. This paper argues that the architecture of the Ottawa Mosque thus operates as a form of eisegesis, materializing and sustaining patriarchal interpretations of Islam via thresholds, sightlines, and mediated visibility. Comparative Canadian mosques reveal this segregation as culturally contingent, rather than theological. I am studying the Ottawa Mosque through its physical organization and the behaviors it produces. Following Annmarie Adams’s approach to institutional buildings, which reads gender through patterns of circulation and visibility, the analysis begins with close observation of how people move, see, and wait. Auto-ethnographic observation grounds analysis in lived movement. Plans and photos trace how thresholds, balconies, and overflow direct gendered visibility, connecting Ottawa to examples studied by Tammy Gaber and Nadia Kurd. Feminist frameworks reveal architecture as a site where interpretations of gender are translated into space. Through entrances, partitions, and circulatory hierarchies, exclusion becomes material and enforceable.

    Choreography of Entries

    At the Ottawa Mosque, the separation begins before I even reach the building. When my father and I arrive, we part naturally in the parking lot, each moving toward our assigned entrances. We both start to walk northwest, but our routes diverge. I take the path along the edge of oncoming traffic to reach the north façade, where the women’s door sits recessed behind the building near the parking lot entrance (Fig. 1). He takes the path that opens toward the main street on the south, a nicer path, since vehicle circulation there is usually blocked off. The men’s entrance occupies the west façade, unmistakably the front of the mosque (Fig. 2). Three glazed double doors span fifteen feet, and broad concrete steps create a landing where people gather after prayer. Inside, a vestibule offers a moment to remove shoes, and another open space follows before the main hall (Fig. 4). I imagine this feels spacious and public. There are even two additional men’s entrances along the south façade, both glazed and visible from the street, wide enough for groups to enter together. The women’s entrance is different. A solid steel single door on the north façade, marked by a small sign reading “ladies entrance" (Fig. 3). Just over three feet wide, opaque, and positioned where cars turn into the lot. Standing near it feels uneasy; you can’t linger without being in the way of the traffic. When the door opens, it leads directly into a narrow stairwell with no place to pause or gather. Movement is immediate and vertical, away from the activity below and toward the balcony where women pray.



    Figure 1 Ottawa Mosque Landscape Plan

    Tammy Gaber, an architectural scholar of Canadian mosques, situates the Ottawa Mosque’s entrances within a longer architectural trajectory. Early Canadian examples such as Edmonton’s Al Rashid Mosque (1938) provided a single undivided hall for the whole community, reflecting formative Islamic precedents where gender ordering was negotiated socially rather than fixed by built barriers. The othering of the “ladies' entrance” aligns women spatially with secondary functions rather than with the core ritual space. Leila Ahmed’s history of gender in Islam provides a deeper genealogy for this spatial hierarchy. She traces how practices of veiling and seclusion in the Middle East emerged through the assimilation of elite urban customs under empires like the Sasanians and Byzantines, becoming markers of respectability and upper-class status rather than direct expressions of Quranic injunctions. Over centuries, these cultural forms solidified into core prescriptive discourses of patriarchy, in which male authority and female seclusion were treated as religiously normative even though they derived from specific historical arrangements. In the Ottawa Mosque, thresholds take on the role of veil: the transparent glass of the men’s doors merges interior worship with the civic realm, allowing men to be seen entering and leaving as visible citizens, while the solid women’s door and its parking-lot alignment render women’s arrival private, hidden, and detached from the city. The building thus materializes Ahmed’s argument that the politics of visibility in Islam are culturally produced; through façade design and circulation, it reinscribes a patriarchal distribution of who appears and who remains out of sight. The west façade is ceremonial, legible, and porous, offering men multiple ways in and out; the north façade is peripheral, singular, and tight, admitting women one at a time into a vertical corridor removed from public view. Adams writes that institutions enforce hierarchy through their circulation routes, using entrances, corridors, and waiting spaces to regulate access and participation. The men’s entry choreographs arrival as a communal act; the women’s entry treats arrival as individualized, quiet and invisible. The contrast in scale and openness is unmistakable: the men’s doors span fifteen feet of glazed transparency, while the women’s single opaque door reads, and operates, as a service access. These entrances work as a performative sorting mechanism, communicating value and hierarchy through orientation, visibility, and material. The short journey from car to stairwell thus becomes a condensed catechism in the mosque’s theology of access, teaching through concrete, glass, and door hardware whose presence is welcomed, and whose is quietly redirected away from the public eye.

    Seeing through the Screen

    From the women’s balcony, my experience of prayer is shaped by architecture and by the screen that compensates for it. The balcony wraps along the back of the hall, raised above the men’s prayer space. Only the women in the first or second row can see the Imam directly through the opening in the railing (Fig. 5). For everyone else, a mounted screen replaces the view. I’ve spent all prayers watching the Imam on the screen. The Imam’s voice reaches us through speakers; his image arrives through a camera feed. My connection to the congregation becomes something filtered, something mediated. I see him as pixels, held in a frame, while the sound comes from above rather than from the room below. Even though I am inside the same building, the screen makes the experience feel distant, almost like joining remotely. The screen is there to make up for what the architecture blocks, but it also underscores the disconnection. It’s a reminder that my presence is secondary, that the main event happens elsewhere, and I am receiving it through transmission.



    Figure 5 Womens balcony with the screen to the right (Photograph by Author, October, 2025)

    Tammy Gaber’s interview with Sharif Senbel, designer of Masjid al Salaam in Burnaby, British Columbia, discuss a glass railing as an effort for good sightlines for all. More interestingly, he points towards community concern that men and women can see each-other, stating this as a complete non-issue because the men are always facing forward, but his reassurance rests on the assumption that women should not be seen. This raises the question posed by Kahera Akel Ismail, a scholar of mosque architecture and Islamic urbanism: “If the Qur’an and the sunnah have not set any gender-based limits on the use of the mosque, then why has it become an issue?” Adams’ description of architecture's complicity in racial segregation provides a useful comparison. Viola Desmond, a Nova Scotia businesswoman, sat in the back of the movie theatre so as not to be seen by white moviegoers, despite (or perhaps in spite of) the balcony being in a disadvantaged viewpoint of the screen reinforced her second-class status through a language of space and vision. By designing the Ottawa Mosque in this way, where women are placed in the back and above the main floor, speaks directly to how women are viewed within the architecture of this mosque. Daphne Spain, a scholar of gender and space, clarifies the mechanism at work: “By controlling access to knowledge and resources through the control of space, the dominant group’s ability to retain and reinforce its position is enhanced.” In the Ottawa Mosque, the access of knowledge and resources is the prayer itself, and the architecture of the mosque forces almost all women to see the Imam and the Sermon through a layer of mediation despite physically being in the same place where the event occurs. This no doubt reinforces the position of the so-called dominant group at the Ottawa Mosque and tells women that their act of seeing is reduced to a flattened digital image, something to be watched rather than directly engaged with.

    The Movement of Bodies

    During Eid, when the mosque is full, the separation between men and women becomes unmistakable. One Eid morning, the women’s balcony filled quickly, and a man, I’m not sure of his role, only that he seemed to be directing the flow of people, told those of us arriving later that we needed to pray in the basement (Fig. 6). The basement isn’t designed for prayer. The ceiling feels low, the air still. It has the atmosphere of overflow storage more than a place to gather, but we prayed there together, making do with the space we had. After the prayer ended, the Imam began the sermon. A few minutes in, another man came to tell us we had to leave. I didn’t know if he was a volunteer, an usher, or simply someone taking on that responsibility, but his instruction was clear: the next group of women had arrived, and they needed the room. The sermon continued upstairs, but we were already being moved out. My father stayed in the men’s hall for the full sermon, but I couldn’t. We tried waiting in the stairwell, but it was narrow, and an usher asked us to keep moving so others could pass through. Eventually, we stepped outside into the winter cold, standing near the car (my dad had the keys) while the sermon continued inside. Phones aren’t used during prayer or sermons, so we had no way to reach him. We didn’t know how long the sermon would last; we just waited in the wind until it ended. What stayed with me from that morning wasn’t the sermon, but the feeling of being moved from one space to another with nowhere to pause. Basement, stairwell, outside. Meanwhile, my father remained seated inside, part of the full ritual from beginning to end. When I think back to that morning, I remember the cold, the wind on my face and the realization that my participation had ended because the building left no place for me to stay.



    Figure 6 Basement of the Ottawa Mosque (Photograph by Author, October, 2025)

    As we already explored, institutions enforce hierarchy through their circulation routes, using entrances, corridors, and waiting spaces to regulate access and participation. In this case, the lack of communal waiting and shared thresholds for women choreographs their exclusion and marginalization, reinforcing patriarchal control through a constant reordering of women's bodies. Spain notes that women have often been excluded from mosques. Though certainly not all, it is historically accurate that many mosques have barred women altogether. Yet during the Prophet’s lifetime, mosques were open to all, without any separate rooms or barriers and restricted in later period.

    In Canada, mosque design makes clear that there is no single Islamic spatial rule for gender. Contemporary Canadian mosques demonstrate this variability. At the Noor Cultural Centre in Toronto, completed in 2003, men and women pray side by side in one hall, divided only by the mihrab orientation, and photographs from Eid in 2014 (fig. 7) show no physical barrier between them. In the Islamic Centre of Kingston, built in 1996, the original design intentionally created one unified space for all congregants, a decision led by women on the mosque board; only recently was a solid partition added, although women are still invited to pray on either side of it. Across these examples, what becomes visible is not a universal religious requirement but a spectrum of local interpretations shaped by community politics, diasporic identity, and shifting notions of propriety. As Kurd’s study shows, contemporary mosques in Canada and the United States are sites where “debates on inclusion and agency of women have increased,” and where spatial arrangements materialize those debates rather than resolve them. The Ottawa Mosque fits within this wider landscape: its own spatial choices are one interpretation among many, not an inevitability dictated by scripture.



    Figure 7 Eid at the Noor Cultural Center, 2014
    (Photograph taken from the Noor Cultural Center website, November, 2025, https://noorculturalcentre.ca/photos/nggallery/album/eid-prayers-and-celebration)

    The Ottawa Mosque makes visible how interpretation becomes architecture. Its plan, circulation, and thresholds reproduce patriarchal readings of Islam through the movement and placement of bodies. The building transforms hierarchy into experience, who waits, who sees, who stays. In its doors and stairways, the mosque performs a theology of access. Across Canada, other mosques show that this is not scripted by the Quran itself but produced through interpretation. As Ahmed notes, the social views of gender in early Islam were shaped by the broader Middle Eastern codes and practices of the time, and these norms have continued to be reinterpreted across history. Islam moves into new contexts as it travels across both space and time, through migration, community formation, and the shifting cultural and historical conditions of each era. In every setting, the religion is reshaped by the specific social, spatial, and material circumstances it encounters. These differences confirm that segregation is not divine law but tradition. To examine the mosque as a text is to see architecture as a form of reading and rewriting. The Ottawa Mosque materializes one interpretation; others, built elsewhere, imagine another.
